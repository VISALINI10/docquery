Adversarial Robustness in Neural Networks

Neural networks trained on clean data can be highly sensitive to small, deliberately crafted
input perturbations. These perturbations, often invisible to a human observer, can cause a
model to misclassify an input with high confidence. The Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM)
generates such perturbations in a single step by moving each input pixel a small amount in
the direction of the gradient of the loss with respect to the input. This produces an adversarial
example cheaply, but the resulting perturbation is relatively weak compared to iterative methods.

Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) extends this idea by taking multiple smaller gradient steps
and projecting the result back into an allowed perturbation budget after each step. Because it
searches the local loss landscape more thoroughly than FGSM, PGD is considered a much
stronger attack and is commonly used as a benchmark for adversarial training.

Adversarial training incorporates these attacks directly into the training loop: at each step,
the model is trained not on the clean input but on an adversarially perturbed version of it.
This tends to improve robustness at some cost to clean accuracy, and the size of that tradeoff
depends heavily on the perturbation budget used during training and the capacity of the model.

Evaluating robustness only on clean validation data therefore gives an incomplete picture of
model reliability. A model can score well on held-out clean examples while still being
brittle to small, structured input changes that would never appear in curated benchmark sets
but frequently appear in real deployment environments, such as noisy scans, compressed
images, or adversarially motivated inputs from fraud actors.
